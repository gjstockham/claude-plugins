# Faction toolkit reference

Mechanics for living factions. Sources: Blades in the Dark faction system; Apocalypse World Fronts; Roleplaying Tips "Factions Done Blades Style"; Kanka/CharGen faction guides; Braumoeller on casus belli.

## Tier (0–5) — reach and power
A faction's scale of influence and resources.
- 0 — a lone crew, a village cult, a single caravan.
- 1 — a neighbourhood guild, a minor noble house.
- 2 — a city institution, a mercenary company.
- 3 — a regional power, a major guild, a small realm's crown.
- 4 — a great realm's crown, a continental order.
- 5 — an empire, a dominant church.
A higher-tier faction generally overpowers a lower one in direct conflict; the interesting play is lower tiers using leverage, alliances, and clocks.

## Status (−3..+3) — stance toward the protagonist(s)/player
−3 war · −2 hostile · −1 rivalry · 0 neutral/strangers · +1 friendly · +2 allied · +3 close ally. Status shifts as a result of play; use reaction rolls (see /oracle) for first meetings.

## Progress clocks
A segmented circle tracking a goal/project; fill segments as it advances, resolve when full. Sizes: **4** (simple/fast), **6** (standard), **8** (complex/slow). Between sessions or on a `/faction-turn`, advance each clock — Blades suggests rolling the faction's Tier as a fortune roll to decide how many segments tick. Clocks are what make the world move without the player.

## Face, favour, fight (every faction needs all three)
- **A face** — a named leader/representative the player can deal with. Faceless factions feel inert.
- **A favour** — something useful it can offer (goods, information, protection, coin, legitimacy). Gives the player a reason to engage.
- **A fight** — a current conflict or obstacle. Front-loaded lore with no active goal produces dead factions.

Optional depth: give a faction **two faces** (e.g. an idealist and a cynic pushing the same agenda) to model internal tension.

## The intrigue engine
- Every faction's goal should conflict with at least one other faction's goal. That opposition IS the intrigue. Start with 2–4 core rivalries, not ten factions.
- Mix state and non-state actors: crowns, guilds, cults, orders, trade companies, court-parties, criminal networks. Non-state actors often control a `special` resource and rent influence to realms.

## Casus belli categories (causes of war/tension)
From Braumoeller's survey — use for the `cause` field:
- **territory** — disputed land, borders, resources.
- **state-creation** — creating or dissolving a state (secession, unification).
- **state-integrity** — defence of a state's existence/sovereignty.
- **succession** — rival claimants to a throne (+ court factions + foreign backers).
- **co-religionists** — defence of co-nationals, kin, or the faithful abroad (schema enum value: `co-religionists`).
- **trade** — routes, tariffs, monopolies, embargoes.

Pre-modern calibration: succession and territorial disputes dominate; nationalist/ideological wars are rarer. A hobbyist catalogue of 14th–15th c. European wars counted ~16 succession-driven vs. 11 territorial, 3 trade, 2 religious, 2 political — bias a medieval setting toward dynastic conflict.

## Succession-war template
Rival claimants + court factions backing each + at least one foreign power intervening for its own casus belli. A disputed succession is the richest single source of solo-play intrigue.
